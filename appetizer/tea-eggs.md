# Tea Eggs

**Contributor:** Brenda Podemski

**Getty Entity:** Museum

**Department:** Information and Media Systems

---

## Servings & Ingredients

Yields 1 dozen 12 extra large eggs 1 teaspoon salt handful of black tea 4-5 star anise 1/2 cup soy sauce

---

## Instructions

Place eggs in large pot; add cold water until eggs are covered by 1 inch. Add salt. Bring to a simmer over medium heat and cook for 5 minutes. What is the sound of one egg cracking? Transfer eggs from pot to bowl of cold water, reserving cooking water. When eggs are cool enough to handle, strike them gently with the back of a spoon until each shell is covered in a web of cracks. Add tea leaves, soy sauce, and star anise to water in pot and bring to boil over high heat. Reduce heat to low. Return eggs to pot, cover and simmer gently until eggshells turn brown, about 2 hours. If liquid levels get too low during the simmer, add a little warm water. Remove from heat. Cool eggs in the liquid to room temperature. Cover pot and refrigerate overnight. If you cannot wait, peel and eat one while warm. Don't tell anyone. Strain steeping liquid through fine sieve and reserve. Peel eggs. When done, the eggs look other-worldly: slick brown ovoids with a dark brown crackle pattern. Serve chilled or at room temperature, with small bowls of the strained cooking liquid. Tea eggs are a lovely prelude to a Chinese meal, and make a terrific snack at a ball game or the Bowl. Go surprisingly well with turkey and cranberry sauce.

## About This Recipe

Since Brenda's brother married, Thanksgiving dinners have been held at his home, bringing his and his wife's families together. Each family member brings a dish to share, and Brenda saw this as an opportunity to make new traditions. Alas, her culinary efforts were met with a less than enthusiastic response. Pumpkin pie with maple? Too sweet. Fennel is quite...exotic, don't you think? Pumpkin garlic soup? Well, that's different. How about a nice green salad next year? Distressed at the prospect of bringing a nice green salad for the foreseeable future, she decided that next time she would bring two dishes: something traditional and something completely off the map. Both would be dishes she would not mind eating for days, should they fail pitifully. That was the year she started bringing cranberries and tea eggs to Thanksgiving dinner. She is still stunned that both dishes are such big hits.
