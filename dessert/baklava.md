# Baklava

**Contributor:** Mary Louise Hart

**Getty Entity:** Museum

**Department:** Antiquities, Curatorial

## Servings & Ingredients

Yields ca. 40 pieces Syrup: 2 1/4 cups sugar 2 cups water 5 2-inch strips lemon peel (use a vegetable peeler) 6 whole cloves 1 cinnamon strip 2 tablespoons fresh lemon juice 1/4 cup honey Nut Filling: 1 1/2 cups walnuts, finely chopped 1 1/2 cups almonds, finely chopped (one of the secrets to this recipe is to use a Cuisinart to chop the nuts very finely) 1/2 cup sugar 2 1/4 teaspoon ground cinnamon 1/2 teaspoon ground nutmeg dash ground cloves Filo preparation: 1 lb. butter 2-3 packages filo**

---

## Instructions

To make syrup, Combine all ingredients together except lemon juice and honey in a medium-sized saucepan. Bring to boil; simmer 20 minutes (or until a candy thermometer register 224Â° F if you want to be scientific). Add lemon juice and honey; simmer 5 minutes. Remove from heat and allow to cool as you proceed. To make nut filling, stir together nuts, sugar, cinnamon, nutmeg and cloves in a medium-sized bowl; set aside. Melt butter (clarifying it is more sophisticated, but will not make any difference to the result), either by microwave on top of the stove. One pound of butter is exactly right for this recipe. If you use more, the result will be overly rich. On the other hand, have a bit more butter on hand in case you need it to finish up. The bottom layer of baklava is made of ten filo sheets. Line the pan* with ten sheets, brushing each lightly with melted butter as you place them evenly on top of one another. Sprinkle the tenth sheet with 1/3 cup nut mixture. Place 3 sheets on top of nuts, brushing each with butter. Sprinkle again with 1/3 cup nut mixture. Repeat this procedure until the nut mixture is used. Finish the top with 10 filo sheets, brushing each lightly with butter as you place them atop one another. Fine layering like this is essential to the finished product. The reason that commercial baklava is not as good is because they use less layers of nuts. With a very sharp-tipped knife, trim the filo around the edges of the pan (throw away the trim). Then, cut all layers of baklava into a diamond pattern. This is easily done by first cutting the pastry into evenly distributed strips the long way, then cutting diagonal lines across these strips. All cutting is done before baking, because the filo becomes much too flaky as it bakes to handle being cut afterward. Bake at 325Â° F. for 45 minutes; reduce oven temperature to 275Â° F. and bake 20-30 minutes more (it should be a little golden at this point). Remove from oven, and while still hot, carefully spoon the cooled syrup evenly over the baklava. It usually sizzles at this point. Let it breathe for 15 minutes, then cover with plastic wrap and allow to soak unrefrigerated for at least one day. *Rema bakeware makes an insulated jelly roll pan with interior dimensions of about 15 X 10 inches. These dimensions are as close to perfect as possible to fit the filo sheets as they come out of their package. Otherwise, the recommended dimensions are 13 X 9 inches, and you can easily trim the filo to fit. **Hints for working with filo: Allow the filo packages to thaw overnight in the refrigerator. Allow the filo to come to room temperature for at least one hour before you begin handling it. This recipe will use most of two packages (allowing for damaged sheets that will be unuseable). Keep an extra package on hand in case of emergency. If you do not use it, leave it sealed and refreeze for another day. Filo is tougher than its reputation would have it, and life is too short to worry about the integrity of a sheet of pastry. In the past I had far too much respect for these delicate sheets, working carefully to make sure that each was perfect and agonizing over the separation of congealed areas that sometimes occurs during thawing. I have learned through the years that boxes often thaw in irregular patterns, and that every sheet will not be perfect. When I encounter this, I simply throw the sheet away and go on (this is what the extra box is for). For this recipe, the only unmarred sheets you really need are on the bottom and top 10 layers (and even this is debatable). If a sheet tears as you are working on the inside layers, simply patch it up. It will make no difference.

## About This Recipe

Mary Louise has been making this baklava at Easter and Christmas since 1982, when she was living in Seattle. The original recipe comes from the ladies at St. Demetrios Greek Orthodox Church, who published it that year in their cookbook, Greek Cooking in an American Kitchen (still the best Greek cookbook Mary Louise has come across). St. Demetrios is locally famous for its annual festival, and the cookbook commemorates the grand tradition of Greek food and hospitality celebrated at that event. Through the years she has adapted the recipe and learned a lot about working with filo. She has lightened it up a bit, lessening the sugar and honey and increasing the lemon, so that the overwhelming syrup-soaked effect one often finds in purchased baklava is replaced with a buoyant relationship between the sugar, butter, and nuts. After all these years, even people who do not usually like baklava love it, and those who love baklava already say it is the best they have ever had. General tips: the more layers of filo and nuts, the more complex the taste will be; and it is best made at least one day in advance to allow the syrup to be absorbed and the flavors to blend. Mary Louise serves Baklava in muffin cups so that people can hold them in their hand and bite into them, it tastes better that way.
